# Agricultural Extension Guide: Solanaceous Crop Pathology (Tomato)
**Authority**: University & State Agricultural Extension Services (Integrated Pest Management Program)
**Document Identifier**: EXT-SOL-2024-TOM
**Subject**: Tomato Foliar Diseases Identification, Epidemiology, and Management

## Section 1: Tomato Early Blight (Alternaria solani)
### Etiology and Diagnostics
Early blight is caused by the fungal pathogen *Alternaria solani* (and related species *A. alternata*). It primarily affects older, lower foliage first before progressing upward through the canopy.
- **Visual Symptoms**: Characterized by dark brown to black circular-to-irregular lesions exhibiting prominent concentric rings ("target-board" or "bullseye" appearance). Surrounding leaf tissue frequently develops chlorotic (yellow) halos. As lesions coalesce, extensive leaf blighting and premature defoliation occur. Stem lesions on seedlings appear as sunken dark cankers ("collar rot"). Fruit lesions appear sunken and leathery, typically near the calyx.
- **Favorable Environmental Conditions**: Warm temperatures (24°C to 29°C / 75°F to 85°F) coupled with alternating periods of high humidity, heavy dew, rain, or overhead irrigation. Spores overwinter in infected crop debris and solanaceous weeds (e.g., black nightshade).
- **Distinction from Look-alikes**: Unlike Septoria leaf spot, which produces numerous small circular spots (1-3 mm) with light-tan centers containing tiny black pycnidia, Early Blight produces larger lesions (up to 10-15 mm) with concentric zoning.

### Integrated Management and Next Steps
1. **Immediate Cultural Action**: Prune and destroy severely infected lower leaves during dry weather to decrease inoculum pressure and improve air circulation. Avoid handling wet plants.
2. **Irrigation Management**: Transition immediately from overhead sprinkler irrigation to drip irrigation or furrow watering to minimize leaf wetness duration.
3. **Biological & Organic Controls**: Apply *Bacillus subtilis* or bio-fungicides based on *Trichoderma harzianum* at early onset. Copper octanoate or fixed copper formulations can provide protective suppression if applied before widespread infection.
4. **Targeted Fungicidal Applications**: If disease progression exceeds acceptable economic thresholds, apply protectant fungicides (chlorothalonil or mancozeb) alternately with site-specific fungicides (strobilurins such as azoxystrobin or SDHIs like boscalid). Strict adherence to fungicide resistance management (FRAC rotations) is required.
5. **Post-Harvest Sanitation**: Rotate out of Solanaceae family crops (tomato, potato, eggplant, pepper) for at least 2 to 3 years. Deeply plow or burn crop residues at the end of the season.

## Section 2: Tomato Late Blight (Phytophthora infestans)
### Etiology and Diagnostics
Late blight is caused by the destructive oomycete *Phytophthora infestans*. It can decimate entire tomato and potato plantings within days under favorable weather.
- **Visual Symptoms**: Pale green, water-soaked, irregular lesions rapidly expanding into large, dark-brown to purplish-black necrotic blotches. Lesions lack concentric rings. Under humid conditions (>90% RH), a delicate white velvety fungal-like downy sporulation develops on the abaxial (underside) leaf surface along lesion margins. Affected stems exhibit dark brown to black greasy girdling cankers. Fruit develops firm, brown, greasy, corrugated rot.
- **Favorable Environmental Conditions**: Cool to moderate temperatures (15°C to 22°C / 60°F to 72°F) accompanied by high relative humidity (>90%) and continuous leaf wetness (fog, rain, dew).
- **Critical Urgency**: Late blight is an explosive community-level disease. Untreated fields can collapse within 7 to 10 days.

### Integrated Management and Next Steps
1. **Immediate Triage**: Confirm diagnosis immediately. Remove and bag infected plants; do not compost infected tissue.
2. **Protectant and Systemic Controls**: In high-risk weather, apply protectant fungicides (copper fungicides, chlorothalonil, mancozeb). For active outbreaks, translaminar or systemic oomycete-targeted fungicides (e.g., cymoxanil, mandipropamid, fluopicolide) must be utilized according to regional extension advisories.
3. **Preventative Measures**: Plant certified disease-free transplants and resistant tomato cultivars (e.g., 'Defiant PhR', 'Mountain Merit', 'Iron Lady'). Eradicate volunteer tomatoes and potatoes.

## Section 3: Tomato Bacterial Spot (Xanthomonas perforans / X. vesicatoria)
### Etiology and Diagnostics
Bacterial spot is caused by four species of *Xanthomonas*. It affects leaves, stems, flowers, and fruit.
- **Visual Symptoms**: Small (1 to 3 mm), dark-brown to black angular lesions, initially water-soaked on the underside of leaves. Unlike fungal leaf spots, lesions rarely develop concentric rings. When humid, bacterial ooze may be present. Heavy leaf spotting causes severe leaf drop and sunscald of exposed fruit. On green fruit, small raised blister-like spots develop, later becoming brown, scabby, and sunken.
- **Favorable Environmental Conditions**: Warm temperatures (25°C to 30°C / 77°F to 86°F) and driving rain, splashing water, or wind-blown sand creating mechanical microscopic wounds.

### Integrated Management and Next Steps
1. **Sanitation & Cultural Control**: Avoid overhead watering. Never work in wet fields to prevent mechanical transmission. Disinfect pruning shears between plant rows with 10% bleach or quaternary ammonium.
2. **Chemical & Biological Options**: Apply preventative bactericides containing copper hydroxide combined with mancozeb (which enhances free copper ion availability against copper-tolerant strains). Use registered bacteriophage products or *Bacillus amyloliquefaciens* biocontrol agents.
3. **Preventative Measures**: Use hot-water treated or certified pathogen-free seed. Employ minimum 2-year crop rotation avoiding peppers and tomatoes.

## Section 4: Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus (TYLCV)
### Etiology and Diagnostics
TYLCV is a begomovirus transmitted persistently by the silverleaf whitefly (*Bemisia tabaci*).
- **Visual Symptoms**: Severe plant stunting, erect/bushy growth habit, upward cupping and curling of leaf margins, interveinal chlorosis (yellowing) of young leaves, reduced leaf size, and massive flower drop resulting in failure of fruit set.
- **Transmission**: Vector-borne exclusively by whiteflies; not mechanically transmitted via pruning tools.

### Integrated Management and Next Steps
1. **Vector Management**: Monitor whitefly populations using yellow sticky cards. Apply insecticidal soaps, horticultural oils, or registered systemic insecticides (e.g., imidacloprid or cyantraniliprole) during the nursery and early vegetative phases.
2. **Physical Barriers**: Use fine mesh insect netting (50-mesh) in protected greenhouse cultivation.
3. **Cultural Measures**: Rogue out symptomatic infected plants immediately to prevent whiteflies from acquiring the virus. Plant resistant/tolerant cultivars with TY genes (e.g., TYLCV-resistant hybrids).
